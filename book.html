The Book

Who's Driving Your Bus?

What if the part of you that knows better isn’t the one making the decisions?

A book about noticing the different parts of ourselves, learning to see from another perspective, and meeting our inner passengers with compassion.

Read the Introduction

Stories That Feel Familiar

Judy draws on her own life and decades of work with people to explore why we react as we do. You may recognize a familiar feeling, a relationship pattern, or a passenger you never thought to name.

What You Will Explore

The chapters introduce different passengers, explore where they came from, and invite you to notice what changes when you view a situation from another seat.

Bus Stop Practices

At the end of each chapter, a Bus Stop Practice offers a chance to pause and try the ideas for yourself. Some ask you to sit with a feeling. Others invite a conversation with a passenger or a closer look at a familiar reaction.

Inside the Book

There's More to Every Passenger's Story

From Judy's childhood in the Bronx to the stories of people she's worked with over forty years, each chapter reveals a different part of the bus. Open a chapter below to discover the stories, questions, and experiences waiting inside.

Getting to Know Your Passengers

01Meet Your Bus!

At five years old, sitting beside her unhappy mother on a stoop in the Bronx, Judy began making decisions about herself that would follow her for decades. Here she introduces the crowded yellow school bus she uses to describe our inner lives. Why do we say things we regret, react to a silent text, or repeat patterns we know too well? Meet the passengers who reach for the wheel, and try a first exercise in noticing who's driving.

02How Passengers Board the Bus

Where do our familiar fears and defenses come from? Judy remembers a quiet baby she saw in training films, seven-year-old Timothy at a Brooklyn psychiatric hospital, and the ways families pass their own coping habits to the next generation. The chapter looks beyond difficult behavior to the experiences underneath it. Its closing questions invite you to recognize which passengers may have boarded your bus when you were young, and whether they're still trying to protect you today.

03Emotional Baggage

Our passengers rarely travel light. Judy uses the image of suitcases to explore shame, anger, sadness, anxiety, and feelings we've learned to hide. Antonio, a man in a loving forty-one-year relationship, finds himself facing an old wound when he's excluded from a family celebration. His experience reveals how a single moment can bring years of shame back to the surface. What might your own emotional baggage be trying to tell you, and what would it mean to begin unpacking it?

04The Passenger Roster

Who keeps showing up in your life: the Judge, the Pleaser, the Fear Maker, the Hider, or someone you haven't recognized yet? This chapter introduces Judy's passenger roster and three ways of understanding each one: the passenger, the energy it carries, and the mask it shows the world. Some passengers look helpful, even admirable, until you notice the cost of letting them drive. You'll be invited to spot your own familiar passengers and look beneath their behavior.

05Why They All Want to Drive

Why does a part of you keep choosing a response that makes life harder? Judy explores the fear behind the passengers' determination to control the wheel, including the Pleaser who confuses self-sacrifice with being good. A friend's experience with a business agreement shows how an old pattern can keep someone waiting and giving more than they intended. The chapter asks you to consider the difference between a danger that's present now and one your passenger remembers from long ago.

06A New Way Forward

It's easy to say we should be kinder to ourselves. But what do you actually do when fear has both hands on the wheel? Judy tells a story about two children struggling over a teddy bear, then explores why trying to force a frightened passenger into submission often makes things worse. She introduces the pause, questions that invite curiosity instead of criticism, and the empty-chair exercise she uses in her practice. The aim isn't to silence fear, but to learn another way of meeting it.

Awareness, Connection, and Change

07Showing Up and Becoming the Witness

What happens when you stop trying to fix a frightened part of yourself and simply stay present with it? Judy introduces Rocky Kanaka, who sits patiently with fearful shelter dogs, as an example of compassionate witnessing. From there she explores the difference between observing a feeling and becoming overwhelmed by it, including why compassion still needs boundaries. You'll practice noticing what happens in your body, naming a passenger, and offering it attention without handing over the wheel.

08Going Deeper into the Nervous System

We can fill a day with work, screens, food, and noise without ever asking what we're avoiding. This chapter explores what happens when the distractions stop and difficult feelings finally have room to be noticed. Judy shares an encounter in which a patient begins recognizing needs he had overlooked, with an unexpected appearance by his cat. She invites readers to pay attention to physical sensations and explore emotions at a manageable pace, rather than rushing to explain or suppress them.

09Metaphors, Imagery, and Dreams

Sometimes our inner lives speak through images rather than ordinary explanations. A dream, an animal, a strange scene, or a recurring symbol can express something we struggle to put into words. Judy explores how Gestalt-inspired work with metaphor and imagery can help us become curious about our experience. Instead of asking someone else to interpret your dream, what happens if you imagine yourself as part of it? The chapter offers ways to try that experiment for yourself.

10Hug Your Demons or They’ll Bite You in the Ass

What if the part of yourself you most want to get rid of is also the part that has been trying hardest to protect you? Judy tells the story of a patient who imagined a frightening snake, then came to recognize the vulnerable boy beneath it. The chapter explores the difference between fighting an unwanted part and understanding its purpose. Even an aggressive inner 'animal' may be carrying an old fear. Readers are invited to look beneath the behavior before deciding what to do with it.

11When Staying Hurts

Helping isn't always the same as helping someone grow. Judy begins with the familiar story of a butterfly struggling to emerge from its cocoon, then questions our impulse to hurry difficult experiences along. She also writes about Woody, the dog who joins her therapy sessions and sometimes stays close when someone is hurting. Together, these stories explore patience, support, and the discomfort of allowing feelings to unfold. A simple practice asks you to notice what being physically supported feels like right now.

12Emerging from the Cocoon into the World with Vulnerability and Truth

After learning to recognize and care for our passengers, how do we let other people see who we really are? Judy explores the masks we wear to stay acceptable, safe, or admired, and the loneliness that can come from hiding behind them. A toy-bus exercise helps people notice where they place themselves and others in relationships. The chapter moves toward vulnerability, truthful communication, and the difference between revealing yourself and performing a version of yourself that feels safer.

13When Two Buses Meet

Judy looks back at a relationship that began at a political gathering in 1968, and at the dreams, longings, and difficult choices that followed. Her story opens a wider question: what happens when two people, each carrying their own passengers, try to build a life together? The chapter explores how abandonment fears, old expectations, and misunderstandings can shape intimacy. Rather than assigning blame to one bus or the other, Judy invites readers to become curious about both.

14Victim Consciousness

Being hurt is real. But what happens when the story of that hurt becomes the only seat from which we see our lives? Judy explores the Victim passenger, the pull of being right, and the difficult possibility of choosing freedom without denying what happened. A patient imagines crossing a bridge to meet the wounded boy he once was. The chapter asks what it takes to acknowledge injustice while no longer allowing it to decide every turn ahead.

15Giving Your Eyes Away

Have you ever decided someone was judging you before they actually said a word? Judy calls this 'giving your eyes away': seeing yourself through an imagined version of another person's opinion. A woman's experience at an immersive movie illustrates how quickly that imagined judgment can become painful. The chapter explores the Fear Maker, the Critic, and the stories we tell ourselves about what others think. Its exercises invite you to return to your own experience instead of living inside someone else's presumed view.

16When the Student Is Ready, the Teacher Appears

Some lessons arrive disguised as people or experiences we would rather avoid. Judy asks what changes when we stop asking only 'Why did this happen?' and begin considering what we might learn. She reflects on role models, the readiness to recognize a teacher, and Atticus Finch from To Kill a Mockingbird as an example of qualities we can practice. This chapter invites you to look again at a difficult situation and notice whether a different question opens a different possibility.

17We Mock the Things We Are to Be

The way we speak to ourselves can shape how we feel and how we treat other people. Judy explores criticism, mockery, and the atmosphere our reactions create, including a patient's difficult relationship with his father. A hedgehog offers another image for the defenses we raise when we feel threatened. Instead of meeting those defenses with more hostility, what happens if we sit down with them, as if offering a cup of tea? The chapter connects everyday language, bodily awareness, and compassion.

Living With Greater Awareness

18Unity of the Passengers

Imagine a bus where the passengers no longer have to fight over the steering wheel. Judy explores what unity could mean without silencing any part of ourselves. Even the Perfectionist, whose standards may have become exhausting, can carry gifts worth keeping once fear stops running the show. This chapter looks at integration as cooperation rather than exile: recognizing each passenger's value while allowing a steadier part of ourselves to guide the bus. Readers are invited to discover the strengths hidden inside troublesome patterns.

19Nature vs. Nurture

Who were you before you learned which parts of yourself were safe to show? Judy considers the difference between our true nature and the defenses shaped by upbringing. Through Maryann's relationship patterns and other examples, she explores how survival strategies can begin to feel like personality. The chapter also asks why setting those strategies down can feel frightening, even when they no longer help. Its final practice invites a small return to openness, curiosity, and the self beneath the defenses.

20What Are You Doing With One Foot in the Past and One Foot in the Future?

Where are you when your mind is replaying yesterday or rehearsing tomorrow? Judy explores how blame, worry, judgment, and attempts to fix the past can take us away from the only moment we're actually living. She brings the bus metaphor into that experience: if both hands are reaching backward and forward, who is holding the wheel? Rather than demanding constant calm, this chapter asks readers to notice when they leave the present and gently return to what is here now.

21The Courage to Surrender and Trust

Letting go sounds peaceful until the Controller passenger hears it as danger. Judy distinguishes surrender from giving up, and responsibility from trying to control every outcome. Stories of a mountain climber, a 'God bag,' and the saying about trusting God while tying up your camel bring the question into everyday life. What is yours to do, and what are you still gripping because uncertainty feels unbearable? The Bus Stop Practice invites you to examine that line for yourself.

22Moving Forward

After so much work inside our own bus, what does compassion look like when someone else is in pain? Judy recalls a frightening emergency-room experience and examines the difference between staying present with another person's suffering, getting swept into it, and pulling away. She also returns to the parts of ourselves we've kept at a distance. The chapter explores how curiosity, communication, and compassion can help us be with others without abandoning ourselves.

23Living Your Truth

'This is me today.' It sounds simple. Why can those words be so hard to say? Judy explores the difference between living honestly and presenting an acceptable version of ourselves. An encounter between a father and son shows how much can change when two people see each other in the present instead of through years of old defenses. The chapter invites readers to notice where they perform confidence or vulnerability, and what it might feel like to be seen as they are.

24Joy as a Way of Being

Can joy be something other than a reward for finally getting everything right? Judy explores gratitude and appreciation as ways of meeting life more fully, including the difficult parts we might prefer to erase. She questions our habit of dividing every experience into good or bad, success or failure, before we've truly felt it. This chapter invites readers to stop measuring their worth against an imagined finish line and notice whether joy is already available in ordinary moments.

25From Lost to Finding Me

Judy returns to the five-year-old girl on the Bronx stoop and the question that began the book. After meeting the passengers, witnessing their fears, and exploring relationships, trust, truth, and joy, what has actually changed? This closing chapter reflects on integration, the return of the True Self to the wheel, and the possibility of carrying those discoveries into everyday life. It also looks ahead to the challenge of bringing two buses together. The ending is an invitation to keep noticing, not a promise of perfection.

These previews draw on Judy's v13 manuscript. Chapter content may change as the book is revised.
